Where America’s EV charging deserts are, and who they leave behind.

A nine-chapter Tableau story asking why electric cars took off in some states and stalled in others, and whether chargers, money or policy explain the gap.

Format
Tableau story, 9 chapters
Scope
All 50 states + DC, county maps
Built for
Data Visualization, USF Muma College of Business
Live
Tableau Public ↗

01 — The questionIs the EV gap about chargers, income or policy?

Charging stations in the U.S. multiplied after 2020, spreading from a few coastal states to the whole country. Adoption did not follow evenly. I built this story to separate the factors people usually lump together: infrastructure, household income, how clean the local grid is, and state incentives. Then I mapped who is left out.

02 — What the data saysFive findings

  1. Chargers matter, but they are not the whole story. Adoption and charger density are moderately correlated. California leads both, at about 320 EVs per 10,000 people. Vermont has the most chargers per person but only moderate adoption.
  2. Income helps; a clean grid helps less than you would think. California, Washington and DC pair high income with high adoption, but New Jersey and Hawaii adopt strongly without top incomes. South Dakota and Nebraska generate a lot of renewable power and still adopt slowly.
  3. Charging deserts are mostly rural. Rural counties across the Midwest and Southeast dominate the desert map. Alaska, Mississippi and Oklahoma are flagged at state level. Urban California and the Northeast corridor are well served.
  4. Money moves the needle. States with stronger rebates and tax credits (California, Colorado, Oregon) show higher adoption. States with little or none (Mississippi, West Virginia) show the lowest.
  5. Infrastructure grants seem to pay off. States that fund charging grants tend to have more stations per person.
The takeawayBuilding chargers alone doesn’t close the gap. The states that did best combined infrastructure with incentives that make the first purchase possible.

03 — Where AI comes in nextFrom describing the gap to predicting it

The story ends with the question I would most like to answer next: can a model predict a state’s or county’s EV adoption from incentives, infrastructure, demographics and urbanisation together? That would turn this from a description into a planning tool. It could rank which charging-desert counties would gain most from the next station grant.

Read with care. These are correlations across states, not causal effects. Wealthier, denser states tend to have both incentives and chargers, so the factors overlap.
